## If you read one page, read this one

HireLens is software for recruiters. It does not do what you are expecting.

The category it is entering — AI hiring tools — is currently the loudest, most homogeneous corner of enterprise software. Every product in it makes the same three promises with the same three words, on the same purple gradient, over the same abstract neural mesh. The reader has seen it forty times this year and has learned to skip it.

**We are not going to be quieter than them as a style choice. We are going to be quieter because the product is about something they are not selling.**

Their product is speed: screen 500 resumes in 30 seconds. Ours is **clarity at the moment of decision** — the difference between a recruiter who is guessing confidently and one who can tell you exactly what they know, what they don't, and why they chose the person they chose. That is a slower, more serious, more consequential thing, and the design has to carry it.

So: no gradients, no glow, no glass, no orbs, no robots, no stock photography of diverse teams high-fiving in a bright office. The homepage is nearly monochrome, almost entirely still, and it shows real screens with real data — including, deliberately, a screen where the system admits it doesn't know enough.

**If a generated concept would work unchanged on a competitor's site with the copy swapped out, it is wrong, no matter how well made it is.** That single test will kill most first attempts. It should.

# 1. Creative Vision

## What visitors should feel

**Recognition, then relief, then verification.** In that order, and the order is not negotiable.

**Recognition** is the first three seconds. Not curiosity, not intrigue — the specific jolt of seeing your own unarticulated problem stated better than you would have stated it. The reader is a recruiter who is sixty-one days into an open req with a hiring manager pushing and four candidates decaying. They should feel that the person who wrote this page has done their job.

**Relief** is the next twenty seconds, and it is the beat everyone gets wrong. The reader arrives carrying skepticism from every AI hiring pitch they've seen this year. That skepticism is a wall. **No capability claim gets through it.** The wall comes down not by promising more, but by showing something no competitor will show — a system saying *I don't have enough here to be sure, and here is specifically what's missing.*

**Verification** is the middle of the page, and it's where the product actually gets sold. The reader follows a conclusion back to the exact sentence that produced it. They check it. It holds. Nobody talks them out of that afterwards.

## What they should remember

Ideally one thing, and it should be a *sensation* rather than a claim: **the feeling of something blurred coming into focus.**

If they remember a feature, we've built a features page. If they remember a phrase, that's good. If they remember that this was the one that admitted what it didn't know — that's the win, and it's the memory a competitor cannot dislodge.

## The room this brand lives in

Not a launch keynote. **A well-lit room where someone competent is reading carefully and is not in a hurry, even though they are behind.**

That last clause is the whole design problem. The user *is* in a hurry. Our job is to be the one thing in their afternoon that is not adding pressure. Every decision below — the stillness, the restraint, the quiet borders, the refusal to celebrate — comes from that.

# 2. Brand Expression

## How HireLens should look

**Composed. Precise. Unhurried. Slightly serious.**

Concretely, that means:

**Nearly monochrome.** A slightly cool neutral carries almost everything — text, backgrounds, borders, structure. Colour appears only where it means something: one accent for the action you should take, four semantic colours for states, and nothing else. A page with nine coloured elements has no colour.

**Border-first, not shadow-first.** Boundaries are stated with hairlines, not implied with shadows. This is the single most identifiable structural choice in the system and it comes from the product: we sell precision, and a border states a boundary exactly where a shadow only suggests one. Three levels of depth exist. Cards are not one of them — cards sit flat on the page.

**Typographically driven.** With no decoration to hide behind, the entire quality signal is measure, rhythm, weight, and spacing. One sans family. No display face. Headings are *large*, not *different*.

**Dense where it matters.** Real product screens shown at real density, with real-length names and missing fields and hundreds of rows. Not a simplified marketing mockup with four tidy rows — that reads as a toy and sets an expectation the product violates at first login.

**Still.** Almost nothing moves. This is covered in §5, but it belongs here too, because stillness is a brand attribute for us and not merely a motion policy.

## How HireLens should not look

**Not futuristic.** The future-of-hiring register is the loudest thing in this category and it signals the opposite of what we need. Recruiters do not want a glimpse of tomorrow. They want to make Thursday's decision correctly.

**Not friendly.** No mascots, no rounded pills, no whimsy, no emoji, no exclamation points. We are the interface where a person's candidacy is assessed. Playfulness there is not charming; it is tone-deaf.

**Not clinical either.** Composure is not coldness. The warmth comes from taking the reader's problem seriously and being brief with their time — not from soft corners.

**Not technical-cosplay.** No terminal aesthetics, no monospace-as-personality, no matrix rain, no code screenshots to signal sophistication. Monospace appears only for genuine machine values.

**Not expensive-looking in the obvious ways.** No gradient meshes, no glass, no glow, no 3D. These are cheap in effort and high in immediate impact, which is exactly why everyone uses them and why they date on an eighteen-month schedule.

## The uncomfortable part, stated up front

**We will look plainer than our competitors, and this is going to feel wrong for about eight seconds in any side-by-side.**

That trade is on the record and approved (`DDL-VIS-001`). We are optimising for the demo's second half — minute four, not second eight.

**But the corollary matters more for you, Stitch:** with no decoration to hide behind, our execution bar is *higher*, not lower. Plain done imprecisely is not restraint — it is unfinished. This system is unforgiving of misalignment, inconsistent spacing, and lazy typography, because those are the exact things decoration usually conceals. **A concept that is minimal and sloppy is worse than one that is decorated and tight.**

# 3. Homepage Narrative

Nine scenes. One story. The full storyboard is authoritative — this is the narrative summary you need to design against.

**The spine:** one candidate appears in Scene 03 indistinguishable from 346 others, and by Scene 07 she has a decision attached to her that someone could defend a year later. She is present in every scene between. That continuity is what makes five sections read as one continuous thought instead of five feature blocks.

---

**Scene 01 — The Sentence**
*Recognition.* One sentence that names the reader's Tuesday better than they would name it. Nothing else on screen competes with it. Completely still. The CTA exists but is subordinate — its job is to catch the small number of readers already convinced, not to convert anyone.

**Scene 02 — The Admission**
*Relief.* We show a low-confidence read: the system saying it doesn't know enough, and naming exactly what's missing. This is the page's most counterintuitive move and its most important one. The reader expected a pitch and got a limitation. **This is the only scene a scanner reliably reads, so it must work standalone.**

**Scene 03 — The Pile**
*Dread, familiar.* 347 applications at real density. Everything looks the same. This is where "chaos" lives — as a genuinely overloaded queue, not as a visual effect. No falling paper, no storm, no motion. Its stillness is what makes it read as a real screen instead of an illustration of one.

**Scene 04 — Resolution**
*Release.* The same queue, separated. **Not filtered — resolved.** Nothing was discarded; the four that matter became distinguishable. The reader can change what they're looking for and watch the separation reorganise, which quietly proves the bar is theirs and we don't have opinions about who is good in the abstract.

**Scene 05 — The Trail**
*Verification.* The load-bearing scene. The reader clicks a claim and lands on the exact sentence in the exact document that produced it. Not a summary of the source — the source. This is the longest hold on the page and the most interactive. It is also where the product is actually sold.

**Scene 06 — What We Don't Know**
*Trust, arriving as a small shock.* Momentum is building toward "this is impressive" and the page deliberately breaks it: here is what we cannot see, what we will not decide, where a human must be in the loop. Presented as commitments, not caveats. This interruption is what makes Scenes 03–05 credible retroactively.

**Scene 07 — The Record**
*Arrival.* What the reader walks away with: not a score, not a shortlist — a decision record with the reasoning, the evidence, and the uncertainty that existed at the time. The metaphor closing on itself: they gained resolution, and reached resolution.

**Scene 08 — The Room**
*Reassurance.* Security, legal, the hostile hiring manager, the 400 candidates in the old system. Answered plainly enough that the reader can repeat it accurately to a colleague when we are not there — which is the actual test, because that is exactly what will happen.

**Scene 09 — The Close**
*Resolve.* One action, labelled by what happens. The page returns to the stillness it opened in. No urgency, no countdown, no exit-intent modal. A page that argues for careful judgement and closes with a countdown has refuted itself.

---

**Why it feels inevitable rather than assembled:** every scene ends on a question the reader is now actually asking, and the next opens on its answer. *Fine, but how are you different?* → *That's a nice gesture, what do you do?* → *How do you get from that to something usable?* → *How do I know it's right?* → *What about the parts you got wrong?* The page cannot be reordered without breaking.

# 4. Visual Direction

## Mood

**A quiet room with good light.** Nothing is dramatic. Nothing is dark for atmosphere. Nothing glows.

The nearest analogue is a well-made measuring instrument photographed on a plain surface — the appearance is entirely downstream of function, and the beauty is a byproduct of that discipline rather than an applied layer.

## Density

**This is the most distinctive rhythm decision on the page, and the one most likely to be flattened by a generated concept.**

The page breathes unevenly, on purpose:

```
Sparse → Medium → DENSE · DENSE · DENSE → Medium · Medium · Medium → Sparse
  01       02        03      04      05      06       07       08      09
```

One sustained dense passage in the middle, bracketed by sparse ends. **Emotional passages run sparse; evidentiary passages run dense.** The transition between them is itself a tool — moving from sparse to dense signals *now I'm showing you the real thing*, which is exactly the beat needed between Scene 02 and Scene 03.

**Do not even out the density.** A uniformly airy page reads as having four sentences of content. A uniformly dense one reads as unedited. The unevenness is the composition.

## Scale

**Restrained, with one exception.** The hero sentence may be large because it is the single most important sentence on the site. **A mid-page heading at the same scale has told the reader those two things are equally important, which is never true.**

Large type is a claim. We make it once.

## Typography feel

**Neutral, precise, and slightly tight.** The feel to aim for is *a serious document that someone cared about* — not a magazine, not a startup deck, not a terminal.

Prose never runs full-width, regardless of available space. Numbers are tabular and right-aligned in any vertical set — this is a small thing that does more for the perception of rigour than any other single choice. Everything is sentence case; no all-caps labels, no Title Case headings.

## Surface feel

**Flat, delineated, grounded.** Surfaces are separated by hairlines and by space, not by elevation. Panels are structural regions with edges, not floating objects.

Three depth levels exist and only two of them have shadow: things that persist above content because they outlive it, and things that have interrupted you. **Everything else — including every card — sits on the ground.**

## Lighting

**One light source, high and slightly forward, consistent everywhere.** Shadows read as light behaviour, not as glow: soft, low, largely achromatic. **A shadow you consciously notice is too strong.** There is no rim light, no colour cast, no bloom, no ambient occlusion drama.

## Depth

Depth answers exactly one question — *what is on top of what, and why* — and it is a semantic system, not a decorative one. If a concept uses depth to make something feel important, it has broken the system. Importance is carried by weight, scale, spacing, and position.

## Texture

**None.** No noise, no grain, no paper, no scanlines, no subtle patterns. Texture is the standard way a flat system gets "warmed up," and it fails on our terms specifically: it adds visual information carrying no meaning, and it degrades small text where most of our surface area lives.

The only texture on the page is whatever is inside a real document being shown.

# 5. Motion Direction

## The headline

**Three motion moments on the entire homepage. All triggered by the user. Six of nine scenes are completely still.**

That is not a limitation to design around. **It is the brand.**

## The emotional pacing

Because the page barely animates, pacing comes from somewhere else — **the cut.** The most cinematic thing about film is not movement; it is the decision about what follows what, how long a shot holds before it releases, and what the audience is made to want before they are given it.

**Scroll is the cut.** The rhythm of this page is: hold, hold, *gear change*, hold longer, *break*, exhale. That rhythm is produced by density changes and scene length, not by animation.

The emotional register of the little motion that does exist is **focus adjustment** — brief, purposeful, ending in a state that is clearer than the one before. Things arrive and settle. Nothing bounces, nothing overshoots, nothing celebrates.

## The three moments

They occur when the reader acts, never on load and never on scroll:

1. **Scene 02** — the confidence state changing when the reader supplies the missing evidence
2. **Scene 04** — the separation, when the reader sets the bar
3. **Scene 09** — the primary action responding to being pressed

## Everything else is still

No scroll-triggered reveals. No staggered entrances. No parallax. No pinned sections. No scroll-jacking. No ambient loops, shimmer, pulse, or breathing gradients. No counting-up numbers. No chart load animations. No typing effects. No scanning lines.

**And nothing that implies the system is thinking.** No pulsing orb, no progress sweep, no deliberate pause for effect. Our entire positioning is that our outputs are honest — a page that theatrically pretends to think has contradicted the product before the reader finishes the first sentence. This is a hard prohibition and it is the single most likely way this brief gets misread.

**The reader should feel they are watching HireLens think because the page is organised the way thinking is organised** — evidence before conclusion, uncertainty stated, one decision followed all the way to its end. Not because something on screen is animated to look pensive.

## Why so little

A page with three animations and eight still scenes reads as **composed**. The same page with thirty reads as **anxious** — and anxious is the exact register we have committed against, because our reader is already carrying enough of it.

It is also the more impressive execution. Anyone can animate a shimmer.

# 6. Interaction Direction

## The principle

**Every interaction is a demonstration. None of them are toys.**

The reader should never be playing with the page. They should be *checking* something — and finding it holds. A reader who has personally followed an evidence trail does not need to be told we are traceable, and cannot be talked out of it by a competitor's counter-claim.

## What deserves interaction

Three moments, and they are the three claims we most need verified rather than asserted:

**Scene 02 — supply the missing evidence.** The reader adds what was absent and watches the confidence change. This proves the epistemic claim by letting them operate it.

**Scene 04 — set the bar.** The reader changes what they're looking for and the separation reorganises. This proves the bar is theirs — disarming the deepest objection in this category, which is not *is it accurate* but *whose judgement is this?*

**Scene 05 — follow the trail.** The reader clicks a claim and lands on the source. **This is the most important interaction on the site. Give it room.** It should feel like turning to a page, not like a reveal.

## What should remain calm

Everything else.

Scene 01 has no interaction at all, deliberately — anything clickable competes with the one sentence the page exists to deliver. Scene 03 permits scrolling within the pile so the volume feels real, and nothing more. Scenes 06 through 09 are read, not operated.

**Interaction density peaks at Scene 05 and falls to nearly zero by Scene 07.** The last third of the page is quiet on purpose: a reader mid-interaction is not a reader forming resolve.

## The overall feel

**Responsive, not lively.** Things acknowledge input immediately and then stop. Hover states say *this is interactive* and nothing more — no scaling, no shadow bloom, no colour wash, no icon rotation. Nothing changes size or position under the cursor.

# 7. Imagery

## Photography: there isn't any

**HireLens has no photography.** No offices, no laptops, no diverse teams collaborating in bright rooms, no thoughtful person looking at a screen, no handshakes.

This is not squeamishness about stock — it is that photography of *people working* is the visual language of companies whose product is hard to show. Ours is easy to show, and showing it is more persuasive than any photograph could be.

**And no candidate photographs anywhere**, in marketing or in product. A face rendered beside an assessment makes inferences about age, ethnicity, gender, and appearance available at the exact moment a judgement forms — through a channel that never enters the evidence trail, in a product whose entire claim is that reasoning is traceable (`DDL-VIS-007`).

## Screenshots: the primary visual language

**Real screens, real data, front-on, flat.**

- **Plane-true.** No perspective, no rotation, no 3D, no floating devices. A perspective screenshot says *look at this object* when we need *read this evidence*. It is also the fastest-dating device in software marketing.
- **No browser chrome, no device frames.** They add pixels, carry no information, and imply we are showing a photograph of a screen rather than the screen.
- **Cropped to the evidence.** The most common failure is showing a whole screen at a size where nothing on it is legible, which proves only that the product has an interface. If body text isn't legible at final size, crop tighter.
- **Realistic worst-case data.** Long names, missing fields, hundreds of rows, an unreadable document. Synthetic always — never real candidate data.
- **At least one screenshot shows a low-confidence state.** This is an obligation, not an option. A site showing only the happy path is making the competitor's claim, not ours.

## Illustration: essentially none

No character illustration, no isometric scenes, no blobs, no waves, no spot drawings beside headings, and **no empty-state illustrations**.

Illustration appears where we have nothing specific to show, and its usual function is to conceal that fact. Where the thing exists, show the thing.

**The one permitted form is a diagram** — and only where it explains a structure or sequence that prose explains worse, built from the system's own geometry, monochrome plus at most one accent, every element labelled. A diagram should look like our interface drawn schematically, not like clip art placed inside it.

## Abstract graphics: prohibited

No orbs, no neural meshes, no particle fields, no node graphs as decoration, no gradient brains, no waveforms, no constellation patterns, no "data flowing" motifs.

This is the visual language of companies with nothing to show, and it is the single most likely thing to appear in a first generated concept. **Reject on sight, regardless of how well executed.**

## Mock data: a hard rule

Every number, name, and record is synthetic and plausible. **No demo data** — four tidy rows with short names and complete fields is the most common defect in an otherwise good concept, and it hides every layout failure that real content produces.

# 8. Things Stitch must avoid

Each with the reason, because a prohibition without a reason gets rediscovered as a good idea in three months.

**Generic AI websites.**
The failure isn't ugliness — it's interchangeability. A layout that would work on any competitor's site with the copy swapped says nothing about us and fails the one test that matters. This is the most likely failure mode of any generated concept, because generative tools converge on the most-represented pattern in their training data, and the most-represented pattern is precisely the average we've chosen not to be.

**Purple AI gradients.**
Two problems. It's the category's default, so it differentiates us from nothing. And it markets the technology rather than the outcome — we treat AI as supply chain, not product, and a purple gradient is a billboard for our supplier.

**Dashboard hero sections.**
A full product screenshot in the hero fails the first three seconds: it presents dozens of focal points when the reader needs one sentence. Product comes at Scene 03, after we've earned attention. A hero must be legible in a single fixation.

**Resume illustrations.**
Documents fanning out, paper flying, a stack with a magnifying glass. Beyond being a cliché, it markets the component capability — resume parsing — which is precisely the framing that caps us at a preprocessing step. We are not a resume tool.

**HR stock photography.**
Signals a company that could not show its product. Also imports a warmth-and-teamwork register that is tonally wrong for a product about consequential judgement.

**Robot graphics, humanoid AI, glowing brains.**
Anthropomorphising the system is the opposite of our claim. We are explicit that the human decides and the system informs. A robot on the homepage undermines that before a word is read.

**Random floating cards.**
Partially-visible UI fragments arranged for composition, orbiting notifications, tilted panels. These are illustrations wearing screenshots' clothing — they suggest a product without showing one, and any reader who has evaluated software recognises the tell.

**Feature grids without narrative.**
Icon, two-word heading, one sentence of filler, three times. It is the visual form of having nothing specific to say, and readers skip it automatically. It also flattens: three items presented identically asserts that none is more important, which is almost never true.

**Neon, cyberpunk, dark-tech aesthetics.**
Costume. It signals technical seriousness so uniformly now that it signals nothing, and dark surfaces make document content — resumes, transcripts, notes, the actual substance of our product — harder to read. Odd trade for a product about reading carefully.

**Excessive glass and blur.**
Glass says *ephemeral, floating, ungrounded.* We say *grounded, evidenced, solid.* It also costs legibility and performance to buy a texture that will read as dated within two years. Translucency is permitted in exactly one situation: where the content behind it is meant to be seen through.

**Scroll gimmicks.**
Scroll-jacking, pinning, horizontal sections, scrubbed animations, parallax. Scroll is the interaction users are most confident in; taking it produces disorientation they experience as the site being broken. It also disproportionately harms trackpad and assistive-technology users. Our scroll is a cut, not a stage.

**Anything that simulates thinking.**
Pulsing indicators, typing cursors, scanning sweeps, artificial delays. This is the one prohibition that is a company-level risk rather than a taste question: it is a lie the interface tells about a product whose positioning is honest output.

# 9. Things Stitch should explore

Directions worth generating many variants of. These are where the interesting work is.

**Optical clarity.**
The literal experience of something becoming distinguishable. Not sharpness effects or lens motifs — *separation*. How does a page make the act of telling two close things apart feel satisfying? This is the richest and least-explored territory in the brief.

**Resolution, in both meanings.**
Our organising metaphor is optical resolution — the ability to distinguish things that are close together — fused with the decision sense: the state of having decided. **You gain resolution, and you reach resolution.** Explore compositions where those two meanings visibly rhyme, especially between Scene 04 and Scene 07.

**Calm enterprise.**
A serious tool for serious work that does not look like the software people already regret buying. The unexplored space is between *institutional* (grey, dense, joyless) and *consumer* (soft, friendly, weightless). We want neither, and there is very little good work in the middle.

**Cinematic pacing without cinematic motion.**
How does a page feel edited? What does a cut look like when nothing animates? How long should a scene hold? This is the most technically interesting problem in the brief and the one most likely to produce something genuinely new.

**Layered information.**
Overview, then depth on demand — but designed so the reader always knows what they are *not* seeing. Concealment dressed as progressive disclosure is the standard failure; explore versions where the depth announces itself.

**Precision as an aesthetic.**
Alignment, tabular figures, hairlines, consistent rhythm. Explore how far precision alone can carry perceived quality with zero decoration. Push this until it breaks — the breaking point is useful information.

**Premium restraint.**
Expensive-feeling without any of the usual expensive-looking devices. What is left when you remove gradient, glass, glow, 3D, and photography? Typography, space, alignment, and paper-like calm. That is a harder brief and a better one.

**The honest interface.**
A distinctive direction nobody in this category is pursuing: how do you make *admitting uncertainty* look like strength rather than weakness? Scene 02 lives or dies on this. Get it right and it is the most differentiated asset we own.

# 10. Creative References

**Study the principle. Never the surface.** For each, what to take and what to leave — the second list matters more.

## Apple

**Take:** the conviction that restraint is a position rather than an absence. The willingness to leave a page almost empty because one thing is important. The discipline of making the product the subject.

**Leave:** the scale and drama. Apple's register is *unveiling* — enormous type, held silences, a sense of occasion. Our register is *composure*. A recruiter mid-afternoon does not want an unveiling; they want the room to get quieter. Also leave the depth and softness: consumer-grade radius and elevation read as *welcome*, and we are selling *grounded*.

## Linear

**Take:** the demonstration that a dense, professional tool can be beautiful without decoration, and that speed and precision are communicable through craft rather than through claims. Their willingness to show real, dense product is exactly right.

**Leave:** the dark-first identity and the motion signature. Dark-as-brand has become a costume everyone wears, and our content is documents — legibility-critical, better on light. Their motion is superb and it is theirs; ours is near-zero, and copying their choreography would put us in a register we have deliberately rejected.

## Stripe

**Take:** the respect for a technical reader's intelligence. Depth over breadth. The confidence to be genuinely detailed on a marketing page. Their information design under density is the best in software.

**Leave:** gradient as brand signature. It is an outstanding device for a company whose product is infrastructure the user never sees — and a liability for a product the user stares at for six hours, where it would compete with content on every screen.

## Cursor

**Take:** leading with the demonstration. Their pages show the thing working rather than describing it, and the reader forms their own conclusion. That instinct drives our entire Movement III.

**Leave:** the developer-tool register and the AI-forward framing. Their audience wants the model foregrounded; ours is made suspicious by it. Also leave the terminal aesthetic — monospace as personality is a costume we have explicitly refused.

## Ashby

**Take:** the proof that this specific audience — recruiting operations — responds to seriousness and data density rather than to warmth. They are the closest analogue for who we are talking to, and their success is evidence that the calm-enterprise direction is commercially viable, not just tasteful.

**Leave:** the analytics-first framing and the ATS-adjacent positioning. Their story is *better hiring operations*; ours is *defensible hiring decisions.* Adjacent, and different enough that borrowing their structure would blur our category.

## The honest note on references

**Every one of these is a company whose design is downstream of a clear position.** That is what makes them good, and it is the only thing worth copying. The moment we take a surface treatment instead of a principle, we have imported someone else's answer to a question we never asked.

**Do not generate concepts that look like any of these.** Generate concepts that could only be HireLens.

# 11. Success Criteria

## Reject immediately

No iteration, no salvaging. These are structural failures, not craft problems:

- **The interchangeability test.** Swap "HireLens" for a competitor's name. If the concept still works, it says nothing about us. **This will kill most first attempts.**
- Any abstract AI imagery — orbs, meshes, particles, brains, glowing anything.
- Gradient backgrounds, glass surfaces, glow, 3D, texture, or noise.
- Photography of people.
- A hero containing a full dashboard or anything with more than one focal point.
- A feature grid where the narrative should be.
- Motion on load or on scroll.
- Perspective, rotation, or device frames on a screenshot.
- Demo data — four tidy rows, short names, complete fields.
- Any element implying the system is thinking.

## Keep iterating when

The direction is right and the execution isn't there yet:

- The structure is sound but the density is even. *Push the sparse ends sparser and the dense middle denser.*
- The hierarchy works but the typography is generic. *This is where the whole quality signal lives — keep going.*
- The scenes are right but the transitions feel like section breaks. *The question-and-answer relationship isn't visible yet.*
- Scene 02 reads as a warning rather than as a statement of uncertainty. *Closest to right and most important to fix.*
- The screenshots are real but not legible. *Crop tighter.*
- It's calm but inert. *Calm is not the same as flat — the pacing needs the gear changes.*

## How we recognise "the" homepage

Five tests. **All five, and the last two are the hard ones.**

**1 · The recognition test.** A senior recruiter reads Scene 01 and their first internal sentence is *"yes, that"* — not *"what is this?"* If they have to decode the hero, the sequence is inverted.

**2 · The explanation test.** A reader who finishes the page can explain HireLens to a colleague on Monday. Not recite our benefits — *describe what it does.* That is a much higher bar than reading the whole page, and it is the real measure of Movement III.

**3 · The stripped test.** Remove every image and screenshot. **Is the page still convincing?** If yes, the structure and writing are carrying it and the imagery is doing its proper job of supporting. If the page collapses, we built a slideshow with captions.

**4 · The date test.** Show it to someone in 2031. **Can they tell it was made in 2026?** If any single element dates it — a treatment, an effect, a layout convention — that element is a trend we adopted rather than a decision we made.

**5 · The refusal test.** *What does this page refuse to do that its competitors do?* If the answer is nothing, we have built a well-executed version of the category average. **The refusals are the identity.** If you can't name three within ten seconds of looking at a concept, it isn't ours yet.

# 12. Creative Constraints

**What may never be sacrificed** — not for a deadline, not for a stakeholder, not for a conversion metric, not for a concept that is otherwise excellent.

**1 · Honesty in the interface.**
Nothing simulates thinking. Nothing fakes progress. No screenshot is a marketing mockup passed off as product. Our entire positioning is that our outputs are honest, and a single violation converts us into the category we positioned against.

**2 · The claim boundaries.**
We never claim to eliminate bias, predict job performance, replace recruiters, or publish an unsourced time-saved figure — and we never name a model vendor as a quality signal. These are the highest-converting claims available in this category and we have permanently declined them. That trade is deliberate and it is not reopened by a concept that would be stronger with them.

**3 · The imperfect state appears.**
At least one screenshot shows the system unsure. If a concept is beautiful and only shows the happy path, it is making our competitor's argument at our expense.

**4 · Accessibility.**
Contrast, focus, keyboard, motion sensitivity, 200% zoom. Never traded for density and never traded for aesthetics. This is a floor, not a target — and it is not negotiable at any level of visual ambition.

**5 · Real data at real density.**
No simplified mockups. The gap between a marketing demo and a first login is the most reliable predictor of the "overhyped" narrative, and that narrative spreads through recruiting communities faster than anything we can buy.

**6 · One primary action, no manufactured urgency.**
No countdowns, no exit-intent modals, no scarcity framing. A page that argues for careful judgement and closes with a timer has refuted itself in its own footer.

**7 · The reader's scroll.**
It belongs to them. We never take it, redirect it, snap it, or animate against it.

# How to brief Stitch

Operational notes for whoever runs the generation. Not creative direction — practical guardrails.

**Generate per scene, not per page.** Nine scenes with different densities and jobs. A whole-page prompt averages them into a uniform middle, which loses the density rhythm that is most of the composition.

**Front-load the constraints in every prompt.** Generative tools default to the training-set average, which is exactly what §8 prohibits. Every generation should carry: border-first, near-monochrome, no gradients, no glass, no glow, three elevation levels, cards not elevated, one accent per view, sentence case, tabular figures, no illustration, no abstract imagery, no device frames, no rotation, real data.

**Stitch should prototype:** Scenes 01, 06, 07, 08, 09 — the compositional scenes, where rapid variants are cheap and valuable.

**Stitch should not attempt:** Scenes 02, 03, 04, 05 — these require real product surfaces, and an approximation would be exactly the marketing-mockup dishonesty we prohibit. They are custom implementation.

**The accent colour is not Stitch's to choose.** It remains deliberately unspecified, because it must be selected against a brand mark that does not exist yet. **Generate in near-monochrome with a single neutral placeholder accent.** A concept that arrives with an invented brand colour has made a decision it was not asked to make, and the colour will anchor everyone's judgement of the concept regardless of its merits.

**Judge concepts against §11, in order.** Reject on the structural failures before evaluating craft. A beautifully executed concept with abstract AI imagery is a reject, not a revision.
